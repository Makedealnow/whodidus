Case 013

Defund. Downrank. Deplatform.

Those three tactics are a quote — from a pitch a British outfit wrote for USAID in August 2022. USAID never bought the pitch. Its own files show what it built instead: a working pipeline into the platforms' flagging systems.

Built from the "USAID Files" — 248 internal agency records released November 24–25, 2026. Every document is linked below.

  • The pitch: in August 2022 the Global Disinformation Index proposed using USAID's relationships with Google and Meta to defund, downrank, and deplatform news outlets it scored as risky in Moldova, Zambia, and Armenia — a proposal USAID's Johnny Walsh had asked for, handing over a list of ten countries
  • What actually ran: USAID's Georgia program fed account lists to Meta — the agency's own memo credits its partner with 500-plus page removals — and USAID staff brokered trusted-flagger access for partners at YouTube and Meta
  • The money: about $35.8 million in "disinformation" awards to the usual implementers — and $0 to GDI. The pitch was never bought; the machinery that did run ran on staff time

Who did us

The pitch (2022)

  • Johnny Walsh — Deputy Assistant Administrator for Democracy, Human Rights, and Governance, USAID Handed GDI the target list and asked for the pitch. On July 14, 2022, after meeting GDI that day, Walsh emailed its founders a list of ten countries "that need help before they backslide" and asked for "a one-pager on whether/how we might collaborate." GDI's Defund/Downrank/Deplatform concept note was the direct answer. Walsh had taken the intelligence-community introduction a month earlier and staffed the first meeting within days — and he ran the Meta channel personally, as a named attendee of the March 31, 2022 "USAID & Meta re: Trusted Partners" meeting.
  • Vera Zakem — Senior Advisor for Tech and Democracy, USAID The agency's disinformation lead — Walsh introduced her to GDI as USAID's "overall lead on disinformation." She worked the GDI relationship from day one ("We're looking forward to receiving the concept note!"), drafted the agency's disinformation talking points, and was still planning to "reengage with meta holistically" on the Trusted Partner Program in August 2023. After the General Counsel's coercion warning in April 2024, she backed off: "I am not comfortable in pressing on this further at this time."
  • Danny Rogers — Co-founder, Global Disinformation Index Took USAID's country list and produced the pitch: the August 2022 concept note proposing Media Market Reviews in Moldova, Zambia, and Armenia, plus the platform-intervention pilot. He had already presented GDI's work at a January 2022 session co-hosted by USAID's democracy center, and circulated GDI's defunding research inside USAID in 2021.
  • Clare Melford — Co-founder & CEO, Global Disinformation Index Worked the USAID relationship alongside Rogers — named in the intelligence-community introduction, proposed the first call, sat in the July 14, 2022 meeting, and was a named party to the concept-note exchange that produced the pitch.
  • Office of the Director of National Intelligence — the introducing official's name is redacted in the released file Made the introduction that started the thread. June 10, 2022, to GDI's founders: "I'm writing to introduce my friend Johnny Walsh… GDI is the best organization I know working on the commercial, for-profit side of the disinformation challenge." We can name the office. The file blacks out the person.

The doctrine and the flagger channel (2021–2022)

  • Joshua Machleder — USAID, Democracy, Human Rights, and Governance Co-created the doctrine, then ran the YouTube channel. The February 2021 Disinformation Primer's acknowledgements say it "was conceived and developed by Joshua Machleder and Shannon Maguire at USAID." In August 2022, Machleder relayed YouTube's request to partners: send over "a list of potential organizations" to serve as Trusted Flaggers. USAID was the broker between a platform's flagging program and its own partner network.
  • Rafiq Copeland — Senior Advisor, Platform Accountability, Internews Refused to hand over the list — and said the quiet part in writing. August 17, 2022, to USAID: "Sending over a list of potential partners' contacts is not something we would be prepared to do at this stage." In the same email: "I am pretty sure USAID is currently funding a lot of staff around the world to flag issues to the platforms through these trusted programs." That funding claim is Copeland's own statement, quoted as his. What the file also documents: Internews itself had social-media monitors operating in about 20 countries, and wanted into YouTube's Trusted Flagger program.

The direct line to Meta (2022)

  • Brendan M. Wheeler — Democracy and Governance Officer, USAID/Libya Personally joined a takedown request — and Meta complied. On July 2, 2022, Wheeler wrote to Meta staff: "We join NDI in requesting that Facebook take prompt action to remove these posts and suspend the page in question." Meta confirmed on July 19 that the page and posts were removed. The context matters, and this page won't hide it: the posts threatened the safety of a named partner and her organization. The documented point is the direct line itself — a USAID officer could ask Meta for a removal, and get one.
  • Maureen Nealon — Program Manager for Sudan, Office of Transition Initiatives, USAID Sent Meta a removal request of her own. August 16, 2022: a post had published the full names and a picture of 38 Chemonics staff in Sudan, accusing them of being CIA operatives; Nealon asked her Facebook contacts for help "getting the post taken down immediately," citing "legitimate security concerns." Another safety case — and the file records the request, not the outcome.
USAID's own February 22, 2022 memo: "In Georgia, Facebook credited the USAID-supported International Society for Fair Elections and Democracy for its removal of more than 500 pages, 100 Facebook accounts and 120 groups, and 56 Instagram accounts that were spreading disinformation." A USAID guidance document five weeks later describes the pipeline in one sentence: the partner "will share a list of accounts with Facebook/Meta who in turn will dig deeper and ultimately take them down or de-promote content."

The warning they filed away (2024)

  • Chioma Ayogu — Senior Counsel, Office of the General Counsel, USAID Warned the agency, in writing, that its platform pressure risked being coercive. April 30, 2024 (marked Attorney Client Privileged): USAID needed "to ensure that it is not and is not perceived to be putting pressure on them to change policies," given "the risk that USAID's repeated pointed questions about the same program may be viewed as coercive." Six weeks later she advised against any public readout of the Administrator's meeting with Meta, citing "Hill sensitivities and the ongoing Supreme Court litigation related to communications with social media companies."
  • Kayden McKenzie — USAID, Administrator's office staff Flagged the secrecy call. On the June 2024 meeting between Administrator Power and Meta's President of Global Affairs: "Checked with our technical experts in the Bureau, and they flagged that there should be no public amplification of this meeting." USAID Press confirmed: "we are not doing a readout for this one."
  • Ulvi Ismayil — Senior Media Advisor, DRG Bureau, USAID Reported the fallout when a partner pushed back. April 30, 2024: "Meta broke its ties with Internews when this report was published. Internews even noticed that some NGOs that were previously in a coalition with Internews stopped answering emails." The report was Internews's own review of Meta's Trusted Partner Program.

What was pitched — never bought

Read this section for exactly what it is: a pitch, two internal drafts, and a meeting request. No document in the release shows any of them executed, funded, or adopted — and this page won't pretend otherwise. They are named because they were written down, not because they happened.

The proposals

  • The "Platform Intervention Pilot" — Global Disinformation Index proposal to USAID, August 2022 GDI's concept note proposed Media Market Reviews — risk-rating a country's news outlets — in Moldova, Zambia, and Armenia, then using USAID's platform relationships to act on the scores: "Defund: Work with platforms to defund high disinformation risk media in these countries, including through advertising and other audience monetisation techniques. Downrank: Work with platforms to downrank this content in search, newsfeed, and other recommender systems… Deplatform: Work with platforms to block or eliminate the most egregious disinformation peddlers in these countries." The note also records that GDI's "Dynamic Exclusion List" of "2500+ worst offending disinformation sites" was already "embedded in the systems of thirteen global ad tech companies." Status: a pitch. USAID never bought it — see The money.
  • The "inclusion list" — USAID "Big Tech Engagement Action Plan," undated internal draft The plan's primary recommendation: a private "inclusion list of trusted actors" — curated by outside implementers, not the government — that tech companies would use to restore monetization, ad targeting, and search visibility for listed outlets inside Russia, and shield them from downgrading and "shadow bans." The plan itself records that the idea "has been discussed internally at State and opposed because of concerns about… raising freedom of expression issues." The draft's action sections are partly blank, and the idea was still alive in January 2024 briefing asks prepared for Google. No document shows a platform adopting it.
  • Draft USAID–Facebook partnership — USAID internal draft, undated and unsigned Proposed a formal strategic partnership giving USAID's local partners "streamlined access" to CrowdTangle, Facebook ad credits, and the Trusted Partners program — plus an "annually updated list of countries of concern" shared with Facebook, and an Election Team contact to "flag potential spoilers or destabilizers." The file contains no signature, no date, and no evidence Facebook ever agreed.
  • The Power–Wojcicki meeting — requested by YouTube, April 2022; not shown to have occurred YouTube's government-affairs staff asked whether Administrator Power would meet CEO Susan Wojcicki in Washington on April 28–29, 2022, to discuss "access to information globally; combating misinformation and disinformation globally," among other topics. USAID staff circulated the request internally to gauge interest. The request is documented — and it came from YouTube. The release does not show the meeting happening.
  • Samantha Power — Administrator, USAID Ran the agency while all of it happened — and the files are precise about her part. Documented: she personally reviewed DRG's February 2022 disinformation-response memo; her office scheduled a 30-minute call with Meta's Nick Clegg for April 27, 2022; and she met Meta's President of Global Affairs in June 2024, after which her staff suppressed the readout on counsel's advice. Not documented: any order, directive, or statement by Power personally directing content moderation, the GDI engagement, or the flagger work. The operation ran at staff level. Her office managed how much of it the public saw.

The money

What "disinformation" actually bought — USAID prime awards (USASpending)

  • Zinc Network Limited — $9,318,530 · Georgia Information Integrity Program, from September 2020 The Georgia program — the same one whose partner's account lists went to Meta under USAID's own guidance, with Facebook's takedowns and de-promotions behind them.
  • IREX — $5,457,799 · youth resilience to disinformation campaigns, from May 2021 International Research and Exchanges Board. The award description: building youth resilience in the face of disinformation campaigns.
  • IREX — $5,443,538 · CREDIBLE — Communities Resilient to Disinformation, Central America, from October 2021 The same implementer, a second disinformation award, five months later.
  • Consortium for Elections & Political Process Strengthening — $4,700,000 · ELECT activity, from December 2022 CEPPS — the elections consortium USAID funds through its democracy programs.
  • Internews Network — $4,523,793 · Central Asia Information Integrity Activity, from October 2023 The same Internews from the flagger channel above — and from Case 001.
  • Consortium for Elections & Political Process Strengthening — $3,880,026 · elections support (CENI), from August 2023 A second CEPPS award in the same keyword set.
  • CubaNet News, Inc. — $1,864,490 · on-island Cuban multimedia journalism, from October 2022 USAID-funded journalism inside Cuba, counted under the disinformation keyword search.
  • Fundamedios — $650,000 · "Combat Disinformation in Ecuador," from March 2022 Ecuador was also on Walsh's ten-country list to GDI — but this award predates the GDI pitch by five months. It is a separate, direct grant.

Eight prime awards, $35,838,176 in all — and that is a floor, not a census: it counts only awards whose descriptions use the word "disinformation." Plenty of "information integrity" programs never use the word.

And the Global Disinformation Index? $0. Searched November 27, 2026, USASpending has no federal award to GDI at all — no grant, no contract, no direct payment, from any agency; the recipient doesn't even appear. The Defund/Downrank/Deplatform pilot was a pitch, not a purchase. One honest limit: prime-award records wouldn't show money reaching GDI as a subcontractor under someone else's award. What the record does show is no direct award — and nothing in the 248 released files shows a payment. The machinery that did run — the meetings, the flagging channels, the list proposals — ran on staff time, which is why its most sensitive parts never appear as a line item.

The same organization as Case 001. Internews ran the $6 million Palestinian media program — and a decade later it is inside this machinery too: advising on the doctrine, monitoring social media in about 20 countries, sitting at the center of the platform flagging system, across 62 USAID awards totaling about $397 million.

The question

The stated purpose was countering Russian and Chinese propaganda abroad — a claim, not a destination. What the documents prove is narrower: a foreign-aid agency built a doctrine, a dedicated lead, standing channels into Meta's and YouTube's enforcement programs, and a playbook for getting accounts taken down — while its own lawyers warned the pressure risked being coercive, and its press office hid a meeting with Meta from the public. So the question this site asks of every case:

Is it a core American interest worth tax dollars ahead of everything at home?
